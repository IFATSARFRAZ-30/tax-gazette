Tax slabs

Salaried income tax slabs, with a worked example

How the staircase of rates actually applies to your pay, step by step.

By the Gazette desk·Explainer

Income tax on a salary is charged in steps, not at one flat rate. The yearly income is divided into bands called . Each slab has a fixed amount of tax that covers everything below it, plus a rate that applies only to the part of your income that falls inside that slab.

This is the point most people miss: moving into a higher slab does not make all of your income taxable at the higher rate. Only the rupees above the slab's starting point are charged at that slab's rate, and the amount for the lower slabs is already counted in the fixed amount.

Worked exampleA monthly salary of Rs 150,000 is Rs 1,800,000 a year. That falls in the slab from Rs 1,200,000 to Rs 2,200,000, which carries a fixed amount of Rs 6,000 plus 11% of the income above Rs 1,200,000.
Income above Rs 1,200,000Rs 600,000
11% of Rs 600,000Rs 66,000
Plus the fixed amountRs 6,000
Tax for the yearRs 72,000
Tax per month (÷ 12)Rs 6,000
Share of salary paid as tax4.0%

The full table of slabs is shown on the Tools section of this page, and the salary calculator applies it to any figure you enter. The slabs used are those for tax year 2026-27 as built into the calculator, so please compare them with the Finance Act before relying on a figure.

Withholding tax

Why non-filers pay more

What Active Taxpayers List status changes at the bank, on property and on vehicles.

By the Gazette desk·Explainer

Pakistan's tax system separates people who have filed a return from those who have not. The line between them is the , and it matters because it decides which rate of is applied when you carry out certain transactions.

Where the difference shows up

The higher rate for a person who is not on the list is charged at the time of the transaction. Common examples are cash withdrawals and other bank transactions, buying or selling property, and registering or transferring a vehicle. The bank, registrar or seller deducts the tax, so the person usually feels the cost immediately rather than at the end of the year.

What to do about it

The practical answer is to file your return and then confirm that your name appears on the list. The list is updated after returns are processed, so it can take a short while for the change to show. Do not leave the check to the last moment if you are about to buy a property or a vehicle, because the rate applied on the day depends on your status on that day.

The exact rates change with each Finance Act, so this guide does not quote them. Check the current rates in the Act, or in the SRO Tracker, before planning a large transaction.

Acts and laws

How to read a Finance Act without getting lost

A Finance Act looks long and technical, but it follows a simple pattern once you know where to look.

By the Gazette desk·Explainer

A Finance Act does not write the tax system from the beginning. It amends laws that already exist, such as the Income Tax Ordinance, 2001, and the Sales Tax Act, 1990. Each section of the Act says, in effect, "in that law, replace this wording with that wording", which is why it can be difficult to read on its own.

Start with the schedules

The rates people care about, such as the salaried slabs, usually sit in the schedules at the end of the law, not in the main sections. When the Finance Act changes a rate, it substitutes a new table in that schedule. If you want to know what you will pay, look for the amended schedule first.

Then read the amended sections

Next, look at the sections that change definitions, exemptions and procedures. These often matter more than the rates, because they decide who is covered. A new definition can bring a group of people into a tax, or take them out of it.

Check the effective date

Every change applies from a stated date, and different parts of the same Act may start on different dates. Always confirm the date before concluding that something applies to you now. For rules that are issued after the Act, see the SRO Tracker.

SROs

What is an SRO and why does it matter?

The short notices that change tax rules between budgets, explained in everyday words.

By the Gazette desk·Explainer

An , or Statutory Regulatory Order, is a notification issued under a power that the law itself gives to the government or to the tax authority. The law says, for example, that the government may exempt certain goods or change a rate by notification. The SRO is the notification that uses that power.

Why people should care

SROs are how many tax rules change between one budget and the next. They can add an exemption, withdraw one, change a rate for particular goods or people, or set out how a procedure must be followed. Someone who reads only the Finance Act can miss them.

How to read one

Ask four plain questions. Which tax does it concern? Who does it affect? What exactly changes? From which date? The original text answers all four, but in legal language that refers to sections of other laws. The SRO Tracker answers the same four questions in simple words and links to the original PDF.

Withholding tax

Advance tax or final tax: what is the difference?

Not all tax deducted from you is treated in the same way. Here is how to tell.

By the Gazette desk·Explainer

When tax is deducted from a payment, the law treats it in one of two ways. In the first, the deduction is an advance. You add it up, enter it in your return, and it reduces the tax you owe for the year. In the second, the deduction is the final tax on that particular income, and nothing more is due on it.

Why it matters to you

If a deduction is an advance and you do not claim it, you may pay the same tax twice. If a deduction is final, you generally cannot claim it back and it is not added to your other income. Knowing which kind you have is therefore part of preparing a correct return.

How to find out

The statement or certificate you receive from the payer usually says what the deduction relates to. The law that applies to that kind of payment states whether it is final. When in doubt, ask the payer which section the deduction was made under, and check that section.

Compliance

The Active Taxpayers List: what it is and how to be on it

A plain explanation of the list that decides your withholding tax rates.

By the Gazette desk·Explainer

The is a list that the tax authority publishes of people and businesses that have filed their return for the relevant year. It is not a registration you apply for separately. Being on it follows from complying with the filing requirement.

Why it matters

Banks, property registrars and vehicle registration offices look at the list when they deduct tax. If you are on it, a lower rate applies. If you are not, a higher rate applies to the same transaction.

How to be on it

  1. Register with the tax authority if you have not already done so.
  2. File your return for the year before the deadline.
  3. After the return is processed, check the list using your national identity card number.
  4. If your name does not appear after a reasonable time, contact the tax office or a consultant to find out why.

Because the list changes over time, check it again before any large transaction.
